One wrong diagnosis · and the interface that fixed it

Everyone assumed the model was the problem. The model was the one thing working.

In programmatic advertising, an engine decides where the budget goes — which channels, which audiences, which hour. This one outperformed the buyers, and visibly so. The scoreboard wasn't subtle about it. The lift is the data-science team's result, measured by them — their number to defend, and they can. Adoption sat near zero anyway. What I own is what happened next: calls that were right and ignored became calls acted on.

I got this wrong first. My opening read matched the room's: retrain the model, make it smarter, and surely the traders would come around. I spent two weeks chasing that before I admitted it was the wrong suspect. The model didn't need to be more right. It needed to stop handing down verdicts without arguments.

I spent two weeks chasing the wrong suspect. The model didn't need to be more right — it needed to give the traders something to bet on.

The traders weren't being stubborn. They were being rational — the model never gave them anything to bet on. A bare number asks for faith. Traders deal in collateral, not faith, so they ignored it.

A campaign took two weeks, and a winning model nobody used was worth nothing.

Planning a campaign the old way meant about two weeks of media-buyers reconciling spreadsheets against their gut — and it's worth being concrete about what those two weeks contained. The UK's outdoor-advertising supply is fragmented across hundreds of site owners, so a planner juggled disparate spreadsheets, phone calls, and FTP files just to learn what was even available. Rates were negotiated by phone tag. Proof that an ad actually ran arrived late, manually, on trust. And a small business wanting one billboard for one week was simply ignored — the manual labor cost more than the booking earned. The UK's largest out-of-home advertising company — £400M in annual revenue, 40% of the UK market — running on a workflow with a two-week speed limit.

The engine was built to collapse all of that. And it could have — except the people whose job it was to act on it didn't. The business had paid for an edge that sat on a screen no one bet on. Chasing more accuracy would have spent the budget on the half that already worked, while the half that was broken — trust — stayed broken.

So I stopped touching the model and rebuilt the screen between it and the trader.

I built three things. A recommendation that arrived as a plan, not a pick — the model's raw output was a list of billboards; the screen turned it into a full campaign plan a trader could defend. KPIs on the plan itself, so the ROI case was visible before anyone committed budget. And customization that took one click — because a plan you can adjust is a plan you'll own, and every adjustment was logged and fed into next week's recommendations.

Customization was the one decision. The safe build would have stopped at a prettier recommendation list — explainable, demoable, and still ignored. Letting traders reshape the machine's plan felt like undermining it. It was the opposite. The first time a buyer watched their own pushback sharpen the following week's calls, the relationship flipped from fighting the model to coaching it. Trust wasn't something I could design into the score; it was something they earned by being allowed to argue back.

And human-in-the-loop design unlocked a bigger reframe: buying moments, not spots. The old brief was a place — "I want a billboard on Oxford Street for a month." Once buyers trusted the recommendations, the planner could ask a different question and be believed when it answered: "I want to reach party-lovers on Friday nights, 9 PM to 1 AM" — and audience heatmaps, not addresses, chose the sites. Brands set rules on live conditions (show the umbrella ad only when it's raining). On the supply side, media owners fed floor prices and availability in once, and the system accepted qualifying bids on its own — an always-on salesman quietly selling inventory that used to expire unsold. None of that works while humans are overriding the machine; all of it works the moment they stop.

The bigger story: a media agency became the market's aggregator.

The campaign planner is the part of this case with the famous number, but it was one system of five. Over six years the engagement moved the client from an advertising media agency running on Excel sheets and phone calls to an adtech company — the market's aggregator, positioned as a DSP with its own demand side and its own supply side, the workflow end-to-end on one platform. I owned product definition, the roadmap, and the design across all five:

The aggregator
The DSP itself — demand side for advertisers and agencies, supply side for media owners, replacing the phone-and-spreadsheet market with one marketplace.
Audience intelligence
A data platform that analyzed aggregate movement patterns to profile audiences, then profiled every billboard against them — so a campaign reached the right people at the hours they were actually near a site.
Creative management
One library for the creative itself — versioned, scheduled, and served to the right screens.
Display reporting
Proof-of-play reporting — evidence that an ad actually ran, replacing the late, manual, on-trust version.
Inventory SaaS — free
Media owners got inventory-management software for nothing; in return their inventory flowed into the aggregator. The supply side recruited itself — the giveaway was the growth loop.

Each system feeds the next: the free SaaS brings the supply, the intelligence platform prices and profiles it, the planner turns it into plans a trader will act on, creative management fills the screens, and reporting closes the loop with proof. That end-to-end loop — not any single screen — is what turned a two-week phone workflow into a three-hour one, at the company that now leads the UK market.

What it took to admit: the smartest thing on the screen wasn't the model.

The model never changed. The humans finally bet on it.

"Make the model smarter" is the answer that protects the data-science team and the budget. "They don't trust it" points the finger at the interface — which was the actual ceiling.

Where this wouldn’t transfer

The model was already accurate — the gap I closed was trust, not accuracy. If your model is genuinely wrong, no interface will save it; that’s an upstream fix, and I’d tell you so before taking the brief.

The principle it left me with.

When a model is right and nobody acts on it, the problem is almost never accuracy. It's that the model hands down a verdict and keeps its reasoning to itself. Human-in-the-loop design — a verb instead of a number, the reasons named, and a way to argue back that the model actually listens to — is what converts a correct answer into an acted-on one.

Design Patterns Used in This Case

The trust lessons here fed the pattern library — this case contributed to patterns now used across multiple AI products:

  • Human-in-Loop Patterns: the machine proposes, the person disposes — here, a recommended plan the trader could accept, customize, or replace, with every edit feeding the next round.
  • Reversibility / Safe-to-Act: accepting a recommended plan was cheap to reverse — customization and re-planning cost one click, which is why traders were willing to act at all.
  • ML Explainability Patterns: The reasoning surfaces and feature importance visualizations that buyers used to understand algorithm decisions directly informed the explainability patterns library.
