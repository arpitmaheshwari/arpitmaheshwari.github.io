Every week the machine beat the traders. Every week they ignored it. Nobody could say why.

The scoreboard wasn't subtle: the recommendation engine outperformed the human buyers, visibly, repeatedly. Adoption stayed near zero. The company had paid for an edge nobody would use — and the obvious explanations were all wrong, including mine. This is the case as it actually unfolded, wrong turns kept in. The docket — verdict and numbers, for readers in a hurry
The traders were rational; the interface was asking them to bet on a bare number. Once recommendations arrived as full campaign plans — KPIs printed on them, one-click customization, every override feeding next week's calls — adoption followed. Planning fell 2 weeks → 3 hours; £69k average media-value uplift per client (mine to defend). The client's CPO, on the record: UK bookings through the platform went <5% → 100% in about a year. The client is named, with a magazine feature and film, on the live case.

Three suspects. I arrested the wrong one first.

Suspect 01 · my arrestCleared

The model isn't good enough

The room's theory and mine. I spent two weeks trying to make the engine smarter before the scoreboard shamed me out of it: the model already beat the desk. Accuracy was the one thing not wrong.

Suspect 02 · the room's fallbackCleared

Traders resist change

Comforting, unfalsifiable, and wrong. These were people paid to find edges. Watch them work — I did, across planners, sellers and creative agencies — and they adopt anything that helps them win. They weren't resisting change. They were resisting faith.

Suspect 03Guilty

The interface demands trust it never earned

A naked recommendation asks a trader to stake their number on a stranger's word. No sources, no stakes, no way to argue back. Rational people decline that bet every time — and they did.

The evidence that cleared the first two suspects came from the field: sitting with campaign planners juggling spreadsheets, phone tag and FTP files across hundreds of site owners; with media sellers watching unsold weeks expire worthless; with a £400M company — 40% of the UK's out-of-home market — running on a two-week speed limit. Nobody in that ecosystem had room for faith. Everybody had room for a better bet.

So I stopped interrogating the model and searched the handover instead. The question was never "how do we make it more right?" It was "what would a trader need on the table before staking their own number on it?"

The mechanism: a plan you can argue with.

Three moves, shipped as one screen. The model's raw output — a ranked list of billboards — became a full campaign plan: reach, budget, formats, locations. The KPIs it would be judged on were printed on the plan itself, so the ROI case stood in the open before anyone committed budget. And customization took one click — every adjustment logged and fed into the following week's recommendations, so pushing back on the machine visibly made it sharper.

The first time a buyer watched their own override sharpen the next week's calls, the relationship flipped — from fighting the machine to coaching it. Trust wasn't a feeling I designed into a score. It was collateral the system posted, week after week, until betting on it was the rational move.

The aftermath, in two ledgers.

Measured inside the engagement — mine to defend: campaign planning 2 weeks → 3 hours once traders bet on the model · £69,000 average media-value uplift per client · 45% less time-and-effort to plan and book · 3× purchase-intent and 70% audience uplift over traditional bookings.

On the public record — the client's leaders, to the press: UK bookings through the trading platform under 5% → 100% in about a year; an availability search that took three days now takes 30 seconds; audience-optimised campaigns +53% consideration. Their words, quoted and linked from the live case — never re-badged as mine. The bidding model and its 50M-bids-per-hour infrastructure are engineering's win, and they own it.

And once the plans were believed, the brief itself changed species — from "a billboard on Oxford Street for a month" to "reach party-lovers on Friday nights, 9 PM to 1 AM." Trust didn't just speed the old workflow up. It made a better question askable.

Case closed. What I keep from it.

The expensive mistake in AI products is almost never the model — it's the handover. I now examine the handover before anyone spends a sprint on accuracy, and I budget for the two weeks I once wasted arresting the wrong suspect. The mechanism transfers only where the user is an expert who is accountable for the output; hand the same argue-back loop to a casual consumer and you collect noise, not coaching. And it cannot launder a weak model — it can only surface a strong one.
