Accuracy is table stakes. Trust is a mechanism — and you can design it.

Every AI team I've met believes adoption follows accuracy. This case is five years of evidence that it doesn't — from inside the UK's largest out-of-home advertising company, where a model that beat its human traders sat unused until the interface started posting collateral. Steal the principle; the receipts are attached.

AdTech · Named client · 2019–2025 · Lead Product Designer, 50+ distributed team

The principle this case proves

Users don't adopt the most accurate system. They adopt the one that lets them argue back.

The stakes The test The mechanism The evidence Where it breaks

What believing the opposite was costing.

The client — £400M in revenue, 40% of the UK's out-of-home market — had built a recommendation engine that beat its own traders on a public scoreboard. The house belief was the industry's belief: make it more accurate and adoption will come. So the model got better, and the traders kept planning campaigns the old way: two weeks of spreadsheets and phone tag across hundreds of site owners, per campaign. An edge the company had already paid for sat on a screen nobody would stake their number on. I believed the house theory too — I spent my first two weeks trying to sharpen the model before the scoreboard shamed me out of it. That failed fortnight is the reason this page exists.

The test: give the traders standing to disagree.

If the principle is right, adoption should move without touching the model at all. That was the bet. The recommendation became a full campaign plan — reach, budget, formats, locations — with its KPIs printed on it, so the ROI case stood in the open before budget moved. And disagreement became a control instead of a betrayal: one-click customization, every override logged and fed into the following week's recommendations. The trader's pushback visibly made the machine sharper — which is what arguing back means.

The strongest objection, kept in

"Letting traders reshape the machine's plan undermines the machine." That was the room's position, and it is the reasonable one. It is also backwards: an unchallengeable system doesn't read as authoritative, it reads as unaccountable — and experts decline to stake their name on the unaccountable. The objection dissolved the first time a buyer watched their own override sharpen the following week's plan.

The principle, compounding through the platform.

Once plans were believed, the brief changed species: from "a billboard on Oxford Street for a month" to "reach party-lovers on Friday nights, 9 PM to 1 AM" — audiences and moments, not addresses. The same argue-back logic shaped the supply side: media owners got inventory software free, and their availability flowed into the aggregator in return — supply arriving without a phone call, on terms the owners themselves controlled. Argue-back, both sides of the market.

Falsifiable evidence, in two ledgers.

Mine to defend in the room: campaign planning 2 weeks → 3 hours once traders bet on the model · £69,000 average media-value uplift per client · 45% less time-and-effort to plan and book · 3× purchase intent and 70% audience uplift vs traditional bookings.

On the public record, in the client's own voice: UK bookings through the trading platform went from under 5% to 100% in about a year (their CPO); a three-day availability search now takes 30 seconds; audience-optimised campaigns carry +53% consideration (their product director). The client and platforms are named, with a magazine feature and film, on the live case. The bidding model and its 50M-bids-per-hour infrastructure are engineering's — attributed, never claimed.

Where the principle breaks.

Argue-back needs an accountable expert on the other side — a trader with a number against their name, an analyst signing an IC memo. Casual users generate noise, not coaching; a feedback loop trained on noise degrades. And the principle cannot rescue a weak model: collateral only works when the house can pay. If either condition fails in your product, this case argues against copying it — which is exactly what a principle worth stealing should do.
