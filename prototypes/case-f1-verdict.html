The verdict, in three sentences

The recommendation engine already beat the human traders; they overrode it anyway, and every override was rational. I stopped tuning the model and redesigned what it handed over: a full campaign plan with its KPIs attached and one-click customization that fed back into next week's calls. Adoption followed trust, planning collapsed from two weeks to three hours, and the client — the UK's largest out-of-home advertising company, £400M in revenue — said the rest to a magazine, on camera, in public.

The model was the one thing already working.

The engine outperformed the buyers on a visible scoreboard, week after week. Adoption sat near zero anyway. I got the diagnosis wrong first — I spent two weeks trying to make the model smarter before admitting the traders weren't being stubborn, they were being rational. A bare recommendation asks for faith. Traders deal in collateral. The business had paid for an edge that sat on a screen nobody would bet on, inside a £400M company running 40% of the UK's out-of-home market on phone calls and spreadsheets.

Trust is a mechanism you build, not a feeling you wait for.

I built three things. A recommendation that arrived as a plan, not a pick — reach, budget, formats, locations. The KPIs it would be judged on, printed on the plan itself, so the ROI case was visible before anyone committed budget. And customization that took one click — because a plan you can adjust is a plan you'll own, and every adjustment was logged and fed into the next week's recommendations. The first time a buyer watched their own pushback sharpen the following week's calls, the relationship flipped from fighting the model to coaching it.

Letting traders reshape the machine's plan felt to the room like undermining it. It was the opposite, and it was the one decision in this project I would defend against any stakeholder, twice.

Adoption unlocked a better market, not just a faster workflow.

Once the plans were believed, the brief itself could change shape: from "a billboard on Oxford Street for a month" to "reach party-lovers on Friday nights, 9 PM to 1 AM" — audiences and moments, not addresses. On the supply side, media owners got inventory software free, and in exchange their availability flowed into the aggregator: supply arrived without a phone call. None of that works while humans override the machine; all of it works the moment they stop.

What it cost: two weeks chasing the wrong suspect, in public.

My opening read matched the room's — retrain the model, make it smarter, surely the traders come around. I spent two weeks on that before admitting the model didn't need to be more right; it needed to give the traders something to bet on. I keep that fortnight in the case on purpose: the expensive mistake in AI products is almost never the model, and I now check the human-in-the-loop design first, before anyone spends a sprint on accuracy. The bidding model itself and the 50M-bids-per-hour infrastructure are engineering's win, and they own it.

Where this wouldn't transfer.

This mechanism assumes an expert user who is accountable for the machine's output — a trader with a number against their name. Give the same argue-back loop to a casual consumer and you get noise, not coaching. It also assumes the model is genuinely good: customization can't launder a weak engine, it can only surface a strong one. If your product fails on either condition, this case is a warning, not a template.
