The Receipt Beside the Number.

Essay

beside the number, in the same line

You lead your portfolio, your deck or your dashboard with a precise figure. The research says that from a speaker with something to gain, precision backfires. Here is what I moved, and why the miss goes in too.

A portfolio is the most self-interested document a person publishes

Keep that in mind for the next paragraph.

Zhang and Schwarz, in the Journal of Experimental Social Psychology, found something uncomfortable about precise numbers. “68.5%” reads as more credible than “about two-thirds”, but only when the source seems impartial. When readers suspect the speaker has something to gain, precision backfires. It reads as a tactic.

I had built a homepage that led with precise figures. Sixty percent faster. Two weeks to three hours. A million a year. Every one true, every one measured, and every one standing alone at the exact moment a stranger was deciding whether to believe me. I was proud of the receipts panel that held the full measurement, one click away. I had not noticed that at the point of reading, the numbers were naked. I had put the evidence where I would look for it, not where a stranger does.

What a receipt is

A receipt is not a footnote. It is the measurement, beside the number, in the same line, at the same moment the eye lands. Three parts, every time.

The Stanford Web Credibility Project distilled three years of research with over 4,500 people into ten guidelines. The first is the one I had never thought about: make it easy to verify the accuracy of the information on your site. Not “be accurate”. Make verification easy. Those are different jobs.

The fix was small and everywhere

Nothing new was claimed. The context that already existed was moved to where the eye lands first. Beside “60% faster”: deal screening, measured before and after rollout. Beside “$1M a year”: recurring, against the 2016 print budget. Beside “2 wks to 3 hrs”: per campaign, against the two-week manual baseline.

Then the receipts went onto the case pages as a ledger, and I made a rule for the ledger: the miss goes in. The PTC case records that my first accessibility pass slowed down the readers it was built to serve. The PlanIt case records that the product was switched off within months.

A ledger with only wins is a brochure. A ledger with the miss is a receipt.

Why the miss makes the wins believable

There is a sixty-year-old finding in social psychology called the pratfall effect: a competent person who admits a real blunder is liked more, not less. The blunder has to be real, and it has to be stated plainly. Dressed up as modesty, the same admission costs you: nine studies on humblebragging by Sezer, Gino and Norton found that bragging disguised as humility reads as less likeable, less competent and less sincere than plain bragging.

So the miss is stated flat. Not “I somehow managed to over-describe things.” My first accessibility pass slowed down the readers it was built to serve.

This is the same mechanism as an AI model saying “I’m not sure”. A system that can only look pleased is not trusted when things go wrong. A portfolio that has never failed is not believed when it says it succeeded. The miss is the abstention state of a career.

A test you can run on your own numbers

The half-second of doubt is the only thing I design.
