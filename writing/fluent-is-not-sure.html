Fluent Is Not the Same as Sure.

Essay

fluent is not sure

If you are hiring someone to lead a product built on a language model, the question to ask them is not which model. It is what the screen does when the model is wrong and sounds right. Here is what changed when my work moved from scores to sentences, and the part I got wrong first.

The old problem was a number nobody trusted

At Talon the model produced recommendations, and the work was getting a trading desk to act on output that was already right. The model’s answer had a shape. You could look at it, argue with it, and change it.

Then I worked on two products where the model was a large language model, the kind of AI that reads documents and writes like a person. One read board packs, statements and interview notes for private-equity deal screening. The other read codebases and design documents for technical due diligence. Four things were different, and I saw each of them first-hand.

What is different

The first is on the record in the deal-screening case: on screen, a ninety-percent-confident score looked identical to a fifty-percent guess, and nothing said whether a claim came from a real document or was quietly made up. So analysts audited the score by hand and demoted the AI to sanity check.

A number can be wrong. A sentence can be wrong and persuasive at the same moment.

What I got wrong first

I thought citations were enough. Put the source beside the claim, and a sceptical reader has what they need. That was my miss. The sources were there and people still did not open them.

A citation nobody opens is decoration. It makes the answer look checked without anyone checking it, which is worse than no citation, because the look of rigour is now doing the persuading.

The due-diligence case says where that landed: citations are table stakes in 2026; the work was making them cheap enough to check that partners actually checked.

What the screen did instead

Three decisions came out of that, across the two products.

The third looks like a small interaction. It was the whole argument. It turns the model from an oracle you either believe or ignore into an evidence file a partner walks through, each verdict grounded in its cited source.

Notice what none of the three does. None asks the model how sure it is and prints the reply. What the reader gets is what the documents support, and a plain statement when they do not support enough.

What it moved

Deal screening ran 60% faster, measured before and after rollout, because analysts stopped re-verifying every machine claim by hand. Diligence cycles went from three weeks to four days. That second figure belongs to the whole platform: model, retrieval, pipeline and surface. The part I claim is the design that made partners willing to stand on it for live deals.

The accuracy in both cases is the machine-learning team’s result to defend, not mine.

How we knew it was ready

I cannot give you a benchmark score. What told us it was ready was analysts checking its answers against the documents as they used it. Their overrides were logged for the data-science team, so a disagreement was not lost.

I would not present that as a method to copy without thought. It worked because the readers were experts whose names go on the memo, and they open references. It would not work for an audience that does not.

What did not change

The question is the same one as at Talon. A professional will not act on an answer they cannot defend to the person above them. With a score, the defence was a reason. With a language model, the defence is the document.

Where this stops

Putting the source beside every claim pays only when readers actually open the references and wrong answers are expensive. Citations can’t save a guessing model. And a lock in front of someone with nothing at stake is a nag screen: they will click through and resent you. Friction is a precision instrument. Both cases argue for placing it, not sprinkling it.
