The Model Was Right and Ignored.

Essay

right, and ignored anyway

If you are hiring someone to lead an AI product, this is the failure I have been handed most often: the model is right, and nobody uses it. It arrives dressed as a model problem, so the effort goes into the model. Here is what it turned out to be on the products I have shipped, and four questions to ask of yours.

The engine beat the desk. The desk ignored it.

In 2019 I was working on a recommendation engine at Talon, the UK’s largest out-of-home advertising company. The engine did something the trading desk genuinely could not: it read demand and supply across the whole market and proposed where a campaign should run. It outperformed the traders’ own picks, visibly, on their own numbers. Adoption sat near zero.

The usual instinct is the engineer’s instinct: if a correct thing is not being used, make it more correct. Here it would have bought nothing. The model was already beating the desk. The wrong suspect is expensive, and it looks exactly like diligence while you are chasing it.

The traders were being rational

The traders were not being stubborn. A bare recommendation asks for faith, and traders deal in collateral: their name goes on the booking, and “the system said so” is not a defence you can carry into a client review.

The model did not need to be more right. It needed to hand over something a professional could put their name on.

So nothing in the model changed. What it handed over did.

The engine’s raw output was a ranked list of billboards. A list is an instruction: take it or leave it, and there is no third move. So the screen stopped serving a list and started serving a full campaign plan: the sites, the creative rotation, and the KPIs the plan was committing to, printed on the plan itself. Every component customisable in one click.

The trader could argue back — pull a cluster, shift budget between channels, reject a panel on pricing — and every one of those edits was logged and fed into the next week’s recommendations. A trader who overrode Friday’s plan watched the following week’s arrive already reflecting the override.

Campaign planning went from two weeks to three hours. Against the traders’ own baselines, the plans carried £69,000 more media value per client on average. Those two figures were measured in the work, and they are mine to defend.

The third is not my claim. Talon’s Chief Product Officer said on the record that bookings through the platform went from under 5% to 100% of UK bookings in about a year.

But putting a name on it takes more than a plan

Talon’s traders could sign a plan because they could change it. Two other products had no plan to change. Both are built on an LLM, the kind of AI that reads documents and writes, and both put a verdict in front of someone accountable for a decision: an analyst screening private-equity deals, and a partner signing off technical due diligence on a startup. Each showed one more thing the output has to carry.

1. A reason, not a number

In the private-equity screening product the model scored deal risk accurately, and the analysts checked its work by hand anyway. A number with no sources isn’t evidence, it’s homework.

I had the fix wrong at first. I used to think explainability meant exposing the machinery: model weights, feature vectors, the architecture. It doesn’t. The analysts didn’t want a lecture on the model. They wanted an answer to one human question: why this, and not that?

So the surface I built was an “explain this score” drawer, so no claim floats free:

The analyst wasn’t being asked to trust a number. They were being handed a case they could check, argue with, and put their name next to.

2. Something to say when it is not sure

Every model is wrong some of the time, and most interfaces have no language for being wrong. A confident number and a coin-flip look identical on screen, and the user finds out which one they were holding only after they have acted on it.

So I changed the order I design in. I start with the question almost nobody asks until production forces it: what does this look like when it’s wrong?

3. One ask, spent where it counts

The easiest way to keep a human in the loop is to ask them to confirm everything. It is also the surest way to lose them. Attention is a finite account, and every “are you sure?” is a withdrawal from it. Past a certain rate the prompts stop being questions and become wallpaper, and the muscle-memory answer to wallpaper is skip.

So the design question is never whether to ask. It is where.

The rule underneath

The same split kept appearing across these products. The patterns library names it the Act / Review / Ignore rule: a score never reaches the screen without resolving to a verb — act on it, review it, or ignore it. It is a synthesis across the AI products, not the invention of any one of them.

Where this stops

Argue-back only works when there is an accountable expert on the other side. Give the same interface to someone with no standing to disagree and you have built a slower list. And it cannot rescue a weak model — a plan built on bad reasoning is just a bad plan with more surface area to defend.

Four questions to ask of your own product

Adoption is not a function of accuracy. It is a function of whether a professional can put their name on the output. Right isn’t enough. The handover has to earn the tap.
