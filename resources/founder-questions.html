12 Questions Every Founder Should Ask

Guide · Hiring · First AI Designer

A model that is right most of the time and still ignored is a design problem, and your first AI designer is the hire who solves it. These 12 questions separate the shippers from the theorists — and I answer each one myself, in writing, at the technical screen.

Questions to Ask

  1. “How do you know the model is right?”

    Look for: the candidate saying the proof belongs to the ML team’s eval — the test that measures the model — reading those results before designing anything, and never letting the screen claim more certainty than the eval supports. Avoid: anyone who vouches for the model’s accuracy personally, or who has never read an eval. My answer to question 1 →

  2. “Your model reports 87% confidence. What does the user see?”

    Look for: the score turned into an action before it reaches the screen — act, review or ignore — with the reasons shown and a way to override. Someone should own where those bands sit. Avoid: “Show the percentage.” A bare number hands the user all of the work. My answer to question 2 →

  3. “Walk me through an abstention decision.”

    Look for: a real product where “I’m not sure” shipped — an abstention, meaning the system declines to answer instead of guessing — and a view on the threshold: too eager and people skim past it, too shy and one confident wrong answer undoes the trust. Avoid: treating uncertainty as a bug to hide, or having no example. My answer to question 3 →

  4. “Do you actually speak our language?”

    Look for: plain definitions, each tied to a product decision: where a claim’s source is shown, whether “80% sure” turns out right 80% of the time, who owns the point at which the model stops answering. Avoid: vocabulary with no decision attached to it. My answer to question 4 →

  5. “What about latency and cost?”

    Look for: someone who asks in week one what is precomputed and what is generated on demand, and treats a slow explanation or a costly lookup as a trade-off to make on purpose. An honest “it depends on your stack” beats invented numbers. Avoid: “That’s an engineering problem.” My answer to question 5 →

  6. “What did you own, versus the team?”

    Look for: a sharp line. The model’s accuracy and benchmarks belong to the people who built it; the candidate can say exactly which parts were theirs. Avoid: claiming the model’s numbers as their own, or being unable to say which screens they made. My answer to question 6 →

  7. “Tell me about a time you were wrong.”

    Look for: a specific call they argued for, the evidence that overturned it, and what they do differently now. Avoid: a failure that is quietly a boast, or one that changed nothing afterwards. My answer to question 7 →

  8. “Can you ship code, or just Figma?”

    Look for: shipped front-end code you can read — a repository, pull requests under their name — and honesty about where their skill stops. Avoid: “I hand off to engineering,” or a claim to the whole stack. My answer to question 8 →

  9. “You’ve held design seats. Why trust you with the product call?”

    Look for: an example where the outcome turned on a product decision rather than a screen, and ownership from definition through to what shipped. Avoid: an answer made only of deliverables: screens, flows, a design system. My answer to question 9 →

  10. “When there was also a PM, who owned the product?”

    Look for: an answer that differs by project and names who held scope, roadmap and release timing each time — including where the line was not clean. Avoid: a job title standing in for the answer. My answer to question 10 →

  11. “How do you decide what not to build?”

    Look for: a requested feature they refused, why, and what replaced it. The test to listen for: does this solve the problem, or re-create what caused it? Avoid: prioritisation talk with no example of a no. My answer to question 11 →

  12. “What do you measure?”

    Look for: whether people act on the output, measured before and after, and clarity about whose numbers they are. Avoid: model accuracy as the headline, or a figure they could not defend in the room. My answer to question 12 →

The best founders don’t hire designers. They hire shippers who happen to do design. Portfolio over pedigree. Shipped over theoretical. Working backwards from user need over pixel perfection.

Red Flags

  • “I only do X tool.” ← Tools change; thinking doesn’t. Flexibility matters.
  • “I wait for perfect specs before designing.” ← Shipping requires iteration. You’ll never have perfect specs.
  • “Users don’t know what they want.” ← True, but they know what hurts. Start there.
  • No actual shipped products. ← Potential is not proof. Where’s the work?
